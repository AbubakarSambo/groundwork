# Groundwork Anchor Entry Scenario: Help Your Team Understand and Move With You

> From Hafsah, 3 October 2026. This is the canonical product context for this scenario. Build it almost verbatim: use these words for screens, emails, WhatsApp messages and the conversation wherever they are given. Where the prototype already has a screen or part for something here, use the prototype's design with these words.

Below is the structure I would give Claude as the **canonical product context for this anchor scenario**. The central idea is not "teach employees about revenue." It is **create meaningful movement from employees experiencing work as their job/tasks → understanding the economic system they are part of → feeling able and responsible to contribute to making it work.**

## 1. What this scenario is

This is the primary entry experience into Groundwork for a **lead**.

A lead can be:

- CEO/founder with a small company
- Department lead
- Sales lead
- Project/programme lead
- Country/business-unit lead
- Manager with a small team
- Lead of a temporary cross-functional team

They do **not** need a formal department or organisational structure.

They simply have a group of up to **20 people whose behaviour, understanding and contribution affect an outcome they are responsible for.**

The free experience lasts **one month**.

The lead enters through a dedicated link that can be shared anywhere:

**WhatsApp → link → immediate conversation with Groundwork.**

No need to understand the wider Groundwork product first.

No need to create an organisation first.

No feature tour.

The conversation itself is the onboarding.

## 2. The human problem

The fundamental tension is:

**The lead carries the whole outcome. Individuals experience their part of the work.**

This is particularly obvious around money.

The CEO knows:

- payroll has to be funded;
- customers have to pay;
- new work has to come in;
- existing customers have to stay;
- projects have to lead somewhere;
- invoices need to become cash;
- costs have consequences;
- delays have consequences;
- the organisation cannot continuously consume without producing value.

But employees naturally experience:

> I have a salary.
> I have a role.
> I have tasks.
> I have an OKR.
> I have a project.
> I completed what was assigned to me.

There is often a **psychological distance between doing your job and sustaining the system that makes your job possible.**

Groundwork closes that distance.

Not through lectures.

Not through fear.

Not by telling everyone to sell.

Not through revenue dashboards.

Through **understanding, conversation, context, agency, recognition and repeated connection to their actual work.**

## 3. The central behavioural shift

Groundwork is trying to move people from:

> **I do my job.**

toward:

> **I understand what we're collectively trying to make happen, how my work affects it, and where I can contribute.**

And from:

> **Revenue belongs to Sales.**

toward:

> **Different people contribute differently to whether this organisation can earn and sustain itself.**

And from:

> **I completed my part.**

toward:

> **Did the thing we're collectively trying to achieve actually move?**

This is **not about making everyone commercially aggressive**.

It is about developing **whole-company consciousness and ownership**.

## 4. Revenue is different in different organisations

Claude must never assume "revenue" means conventional sales.

Groundwork first needs to understand **how this particular lead's world works.**

### Sales-led organisation

Revenue may depend on:

Prospecting → qualification → proposal → negotiation → closing → payment.

The behavioural issue may be that everyone waits for leads rather than creating opportunities.

### Project/service organisation

Revenue may depend on:

Winning projects → excellent delivery → stakeholder happiness → relationships → follow-on projects → referrals → payment.

The behavioural issue may be that project teams believe their responsibility ends with the deliverable.

### Credit business

Revenue/economic sustainability may depend on:

Finding appropriate customers → responsible underwriting → selling → repayment → early intervention → collections.

The problem may be Sales celebrating volume while ignoring repayment quality.

### Subscription/SaaS

Revenue may depend on:

Product value → adoption → customer experience → retention → expansion.

The issue may be Product thinking shipping is the outcome rather than customer value.

### Professional services

Revenue may depend heavily on:

Expertise → reputation → relationships → delivery → spotting further needs → repeat engagements.

### Marketplace

Revenue might depend on:

Supply → demand → activity → successful transactions → repeat use.

### Grant-funded / development organisation

Economic sustainability may look different again:

Winning programmes → delivering well → evidence → stakeholder confidence → funder relationships → renewals/new awards.

Groundwork must **discover the economic engine rather than impose one.**

## 5. The entry hook

The external language should not be:

> Teach your team where their salaries come from.

That's the underlying psychological truth, but it's too patronising and threatening as product copy.

The front-door idea is:

> # Help your team see the bigger picture.
>
> You know what it takes to keep the work, customers and company moving.
>
> Does your team?
>
> Tell Groundwork what you're trying to get across. For one month, Groundwork will work privately with you and your team to help everyone understand how their work connects, contribute more, and move in the same direction.
>
> **Help my team understand →**

For a revenue-specific WhatsApp campaign:

> **What would change if more of your team understood what it actually takes to keep the company growing?**
>
> Try Groundwork with up to 20 people for a month.
>
> **[Start]**

The link can be sent directly through WhatsApp, LinkedIn, email, founder groups, personal contacts, etc.

**The landing page should immediately enter the scenario.**

## 6. The first psychological trigger: recognition

The lead must feel:

> **This understands the pressure I carry.**

Therefore Groundwork's first question should NOT be:

> What is your revenue target?

It should be:

> **What's happening right now that makes you wish your team saw more of what you see?**

Let them speak.

Then follow their language.

Potential probes:

> What are you finding yourself constantly pushing?

> What feels like it only moves when you push it?

> What do you wish people worried about a little more?

> What are you carrying that you don't think they see?

> Where do people seem to be doing their jobs but missing what the company actually needs?

> If you stopped pushing for a month, what are you worried would stop moving?

The lead should feel **heard before Groundwork tries to structure the problem.**

## 7. Understand the lead's world

Groundwork then needs to understand:

### WHAT ARE YOU RESPONSIBLE FOR?

Not job title alone.

What outcome sits with you?

### WHO ARE YOU TRYING TO MOVE?

Could be:

- whole company;
- sales team;
- project team;
- department;
- leadership team;
- five people across departments;
- everyone working on one customer;
- direct reports.

### HOW DOES THIS GROUP CONTRIBUTE TO THE ECONOMIC ENGINE?

Groundwork discovers this conversationally.

### WHAT ARE YOU SEEING TODAY?

Actual behaviour.

### WHY DOES THAT BOTHER YOU?

This uncovers the consequence the lead sees.

### WHAT DO YOU THINK THEY DON'T UNDERSTAND?

This surfaces the perceived psychological gap.

### WHAT WOULD THEY DO DIFFERENTLY IF THEY UNDERSTOOD?

This gives Groundwork observable behavioural movement.

## 8. Different lead scenarios

Groundwork needs to dynamically adapt.

### CEO / founder

May say:

> Everyone thinks Sales is responsible for revenue.

Desired movement:

People understand how Product, Delivery, Finance, Operations, Customer Success and Sales collectively sustain the company.

### Sales lead

May say:

> My team waits for leads.

Desired movement:

Salespeople feel responsible for creating momentum, understanding buyers, finding routes in and helping one another convert opportunities.

### Project lead

May say:

> Everyone is focused on completing their piece, but nobody thinks about whether the project succeeds overall.

Desired movement:

People understand dependencies, anticipate problems, communicate, protect stakeholder relationships and take responsibility for the whole outcome.

Revenue can still matter:

> Great delivery → client confidence → repeat work → reputation → future revenue.

### Delivery lead

May say:

> My team completes projects but doesn't build the relationship.

Desired movement:

Delivery includes understanding the client, recognising unmet needs, communicating progress and creating confidence.

### Product lead

May say:

> Engineers think shipping is success.

Desired movement:

People connect what they build to users, adoption, customer value and ultimately the organisation's sustainability.

### Finance lead

May say:

> Everyone treats collections as Finance's problem.

Desired movement:

Teams understand that documentation, client relationships, delivery, invoicing and follow-up collectively determine whether revenue becomes cash.

## 9. Groundwork should challenge the lead

Groundwork must **not become a system for validating management complaints.**

It should ask:

> Have you actually told them this?

> How much context do they have?

> Would somebody know this was expected from their current role?

> Do they understand why this matters now?

> Could someone do their formal job extremely well and still fail the expectation you're describing?

> Are you asking them to take ownership while retaining all the decision-making yourself?

This creates the second psychological trigger:

**self-awareness.**

Sometimes Groundwork should tell the lead:

> Part of what you're describing may not be an ownership problem. Your team may not have enough context to make the decisions you're hoping they'll make.

## 10. The third psychological trigger: hope

The conversation cannot simply diagnose dysfunction.

Groundwork asks:

> **Imagine a month from now and this is meaningfully better. What would you notice people doing without you having to ask?**

This shifts the lead from frustration into **observable possibility**.

Examples:

- bringing ideas;
- spotting opportunities;
- following up clients;
- helping one another;
- raising problems earlier;
- understanding costs;
- taking responsibility for dependencies;
- asking better questions;
- connecting their work to the customer;
- volunteering solutions;
- carrying things without escalation.

These become the behavioural outcomes Groundwork watches for.

## 15. Team baseline

Before coaching, Groundwork learns.

Questions emerge naturally around:

> What are you working on?

> What matters most right now from where you sit?

> What are you responsible for?

> What does success look like to you?

> What do you think your lead is most concerned about?

> What makes your work difficult?

> Who do you depend on?

> Who depends on you?

> What do you think the team could be doing better?

Now Groundwork can compare **lead reality vs team reality.**

## 16. The psychological journey for team members

The sequence should be deliberate:

### 1. HEAR ME

Groundwork understands my perspective.

### 2. HELP ME SEE

Groundwork gives me context I hadn't connected.

### 3. VALUE ME

Groundwork notices useful ideas, contribution and initiative.

### 4. GIVE ME AGENCY

Groundwork asks what *I* think could happen.

### 5. HELP ME ACT

Groundwork helps turn that into a commitment or collaboration.

### 6. REMEMBER ME

Groundwork follows up.

### 7. SHOW THAT I MATTER

My useful contribution becomes visible in aggregate or, where appropriate, through recognition.

That is the employee behavioural loop.

## 17. The carrot principle

Groundwork should disproportionately reinforce **movement and agency**.

When someone makes a useful connection:

> That's useful—you've connected something happening in delivery to a wider customer consequence.

When someone takes ownership:

> You didn't technically own that issue, but you noticed it was affecting the outcome and moved it forward. That's useful ownership.

When someone has an idea:

> That sounds worth capturing. Would you like to put it forward?

When evidence is useful:

Not:

> Prove you did this.

Instead:

> Great. Is there anything from the conversation—a note, next step or update—that would help Groundwork understand where this now stands?

Evidence should feel like **strengthening the record**, not interrogation.

## 18. Ideas become visible contributions

Employee:

Groundwork:

> That sounds like a concrete idea. Want me to capture it for the team?

Then the lead can see:

### IDEAS FROM YOUR TEAM

Raised by Lucy.

This triggers another important psychology:

**People realise Groundwork gives their thinking somewhere to go.**

## 19. Hidden contributors surface

Groundwork should explicitly look for people who:

- connect information;
- help colleagues;
- anticipate issues;
- raise useful ideas;
- unblock others;
- protect customer relationships;
- create clarity;
- spot opportunities;
- carry work outside formal ownership;
- improve how others think.

Lead board:

**Lucy**

Connected a project delay to a renewal risk and raised it before it became a customer issue.

**James**

Helped two other team members resolve a dependency despite not owning the project.

These people might otherwise be invisible.

This is one of Groundwork's strongest emotional rewards.

## 20. Weekly employee check-ins

These should remain grounded in **actual work**.

Not a curriculum.

Each week Groundwork knows:

- what they were doing;
- what they committed to;
- what the larger Ground is trying to change;
- relevant people;
- objectives;
- projects;
- unresolved issues.

Therefore:

> What moved since we last spoke?

> You were going to speak to James. What happened?

> You mentioned the client wasn't clear about the next phase. Has that changed?

> What's taking most of your attention now?

And then Groundwork lightly connects that work back to the wider context.

As people talk, Groundwork identifies:

**People
Projects
Objectives/OKRs
Dependencies
Commitments
Customers
Decisions
Recurring blockers
Ideas**

Then recommends additional Grounds.

Example:

> You and Kennedy seem to have different expectations about when Product hands work to Sales.
>
> **Would a separate Ground help you align on this?**

Or:

> Four of you are contributing to the Nairobi deployment and several dependencies keep appearing.
>
> **Set up a Project Ground?**

Or:

> This objective depends on three people whose responsibilities aren't completely clear.
>
> **Set up an OKR Ground?**

These recommendations should emerge **because Groundwork understands the work**, not as product advertisements.

## The deepest product principle

The scenario starts with **money because money creates urgency for the lead**.

But Groundwork never weaponises money against employees.

It uses the commercial reality to help people understand:

> **We are all participating in a shared system. My work affects other people's work. Customers make the system possible. Resources aren't abstract. Outcomes matter beyond my task. I have agency inside this system. What I notice and contribute matters.**

That's the psychological transformation.

And I think the external hook should stay much simpler than all of this:

> # Help your team see the bigger picture.
>
> **What would change if your team understood what you understand about what it takes to keep the work moving?**
>
> Try Groundwork with up to 20 people for a month.
>
> **[ Start with my team ]**

That link is what you personally send around WhatsApp. **The experience behind it does the explaining and selling.**
